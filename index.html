RESET India working group — the four themes

These are community projects. Nobody is paid for them and none of them is anyone's next paper. They are worth doing only if the ratio holds: a small, bounded contribution from each of us producing something none of us could make alone.

Each theme grew out of what you told us in the polls and the sessions. Pick one as your primary — more than one is welcome, but only if the first is not at risk.

Click a theme to see what it produces, what you would contribute, and how to enter it.

› 1. Electricity regulation, tariffs and pricing 2–4 hrs
The output

A joint note on why DISCOM tariff-setting resists reform, and what would change it.

You contribute

A structured summary of work you have already done — your question, your findings, the main caveat, and what it implies for tariff reform. The entries are then combined into the note.

How it works: one row in a shared spreadsheet, eleven short cells. Nine are one-line recall about work you already know. Only one — what it implies for tariff reform — needs fresh thinking.

Chosen because institutional factors — tariff-setting, market design, regulatory capacity — were your top answer on the biggest uncertainty to 2047.

Happy to combine together all entries? Say so in the sheet — it earns first author position, but it is not expected.

› 2. Coal transition and regional economies 2–4 hrs
The output

A research gap map: what we collectively do not know, and why each gap is still open.

You contribute

Two or three unanswered questions from your own area, with your reasons.

How it works: one row per question in a shared spreadsheet, six short cells each. Add as many rows as you have questions — two is plenty.

Chosen because the political economy of transition and regional diversification tied as the two things you said were least understood.

Happy to combine together all entries? Say so in the sheet — it earns first author position, but it is not expected.

Open the coal tab
› 3. Energy systems modelling and scenarios 3–5 hrs
The output

An assumptions registry: how our models differ on coal retirement, integration cost, discount rates and demand growth. Not whose model is right.

You contribute

A filled template for your own model.

How it works: one row for your model in a shared spreadsheet, fifteen cells, each a short phrase. For several of them “does not represent this” is a perfectly good answer.

Chosen because modelling here is scattered across five institutions with almost no visibility between them.

Happy to combine together all entries? Say so in the sheet — it earns first author position, but it is not expected.

Open the modelling tab
› 4. Data catalogue — everyone 30 mins
The output

A shared catalogue of what data exists, how to get it, and what is wrong with it — plus any cleaning code or crosswalks we can share.

You contribute

The datasets you already use. Everyone, whichever theme you joined.

How it works: a short form, one submission per dataset. Eleven fields, all factual, no writing. Submit as many as you like.

Metadata only — no licensed data is uploaded. The crosswalks are the real prize: they are where each of us has independently lost months.

› 5. Decision-maker map 1 hr
The output

A map of who decides what in Indian energy policy, and who among us already has a line in.

You contribute

Your own contacts, and how well you actually know them. “Nobody” is a useful answer.

How it works: no sheet and no form — just reply by email. This one is held as a restricted file, because who knows whom is sensitive information about colleagues.

Even a list of institutions you deal with, without names, is useful. So is “I have no contacts at all” — that tells us where the network is thin.

Email your contacts

What you get back. Contributors to a theme get seats at the closed-door session that follows on that topic. Those rooms have to stay small to work, which is exactly what makes a seat worth having — and most of us cannot arrange that kind of access alone.

If the ask grows. These are meant to be low-cost and high-value. If any of them starts to feel like a second job, say so and we will cut it down rather than let it quietly lapse.
